How do you actually measure a double star?

8 June 2026

AstronomyResearch Notes

My final-year research project came down to measuring three numbers for one pair of stars. That sounds small — until you try to do it properly.

The system was STF 1434, a visual binary from the Struve catalogue: two stars that appear close together on the sky and orbit a common centre of mass over many years. For such systems, observers around the world contribute measurements that, stitched together over decades, trace out the orbit — and orbits are gold, because they are one of the few direct routes to stellar masses.

The three numbers

Separation (ρ) — the angular distance between the two stars, typically a few arcseconds. For scale: an arcsecond is roughly the size of a coin viewed from several kilometres away.

Position angle (θ) — the direction of the fainter star relative to the brighter one, measured from celestial north through east. Together, (ρ, θ) pin down the pair’s relative position at one moment in time.

Magnitude difference (Δm) — how much brighter one component is than the other, measured with aperture photometry.

How the measurement actually happens

I used images from the Las Cumbres Observatory network — robotic telescopes you schedule over the internet. The workflow looks like this: choose a target whose separation and brightness suit the telescope and camera; request and acquire images; then analyse the calibrated frames — locating each star’s centre precisely (much more precisely than one pixel), converting pixel positions to sky coordinates, and extracting θ, ρ, and Δm with uncertainties.

The hardest part isn’t the arithmetic — it’s the error budget. Atmospheric seeing blurs the stars, the two profiles overlap, and every step from centroiding to calibration contributes uncertainty that has to be tracked honestly.

Why I still think about it

This project was my first complete pass through the scientific loop: propose, observe, reduce, measure, estimate uncertainties, write up. Everything I’ve done since — including machine learning work on collider data — reuses that same skeleton. The instruments change; the discipline doesn’t.

Details and (soon) the thesis itself are on the project page.
