Why reconstructing the Higgs mass in H → ττ is hard — and where ML helps

1 June 2026

Particle PhysicsMachine LearningCERN

When a Higgs boson decays to two tau leptons, you’d think measuring its mass would be simple: find the two taus, add up their energy and momentum, done. The problem is that you never see the whole tau.

The invisible part of the event

Tau leptons decay almost immediately — and every tau decay produces at least one neutrino. Neutrinos pass through the ATLAS detector without leaving a trace. The only hint they existed is missing transverse momentum: an imbalance in the event when you add everything up.

So in an H → ττ event, a chunk of the Higgs boson’s energy simply leaves. If you naively compute the mass from the visible decay products, you get something systematically too low and badly smeared out.

Why it matters for tHq

In my summer project, the context was tHq production — a Higgs boson produced together with a single top quark. It’s a rare process, and it’s special because it is sensitive to the sign of the top quark’s coupling to the Higgs.

The catch: in final states with two light leptons and a hadronic tau, the tZq process looks frustratingly similar — except the lepton pair comes from a Z boson rather than a Higgs. Since the Z mass (about 91 GeV) and Higgs mass (about 125 GeV) differ, a good mass estimate is a powerful way to tell them apart. A bad mass estimate blurs exactly the distinction you need.

Classical fixes, and the ML approach

Physicists have built clever estimators for this over the years — collinear approximations and likelihood-based techniques that scan over the unknown neutrino momenta. They work, but they make assumptions and can be computationally heavy.

The approach I worked on treats mass reconstruction as a regression problem: train a neural network on simulated events, where the true mass is known, to predict the di-tau mass from the visible quantities — lepton kinematics, the hadronic tau, missing transverse momentum, and so on. The network effectively learns the correlations that the classical estimators encode by hand.

What I took away

Two lessons stuck with me. First, ML in physics is not magic — the network can only learn what the inputs contain, and choosing physically meaningful inputs mattered more than making the model bigger. Second, evaluation has to be physics-driven: a model with a slightly worse average error but better signal/background separation can be the more useful one.
